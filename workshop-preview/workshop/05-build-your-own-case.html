Session 5: Build your own case#

This session is about prompting and creating your personalised Navigate simulation from scratch: describe the case you want, and let the assistant write a first deck for it. The new simulation will be saved under simulations/user/. This session explain the main steps to get a working simulation on first try and re-use the case from Session 1 as an example.

Before this session, we recommend working through the previous workshop sessions and the tutorials, so that you can understand and check the results of the deck your AI assistant builds.

This part of the workshop needs Navigate installed on your own machine. If you have not set that up yet, follow the installation steps in the README, or the more detailed Getting started tutorial, first.

You also need an AI coding assistant to be installed on your machine: one that can read the files in your Navigate folder and run commands in a terminal, such as Claude Code, GitHub Copilot in agent mode in VS Code, or Cursor. A chat in a web browser (Claude or ChatGPT on their websites) cannot do this: it cannot see the data library or run a deck.

Disclaimer

A prompt gets you a structure, not an answer. Each full prompt in the prompt bank was tested by handing it to an AI assistant with no other instructions and running the deck it produced: all of them ran without errors on the first attempt. But “it runs” is a statement about the syntax, not about the case.

Before you interpret a result:

Know where every input came from. Open the .inc files the assistant wrote and check the numbers. An assistant will happily produce a deck full of plausible numbers, and plausible numbers give plausible, but possibly wrong answers.

Most numbers in the assumptions folder are world averages, which may not match your case. For example, all 23 operational profiles in assumptions/defaults/installation/Route/ are defined per vessel class, not per geography: operational_profile_tanker_127k_dwt carries the average speeds, time at sea and laden/ballast split of the world’s Suezmax fleet. None of them contains a distance, and the only two ports they know are port_global and port_europe, abstractions for regulatory scope, not places on a map. The operating pattern underneath is a global class average (which you can change for your case if necessary). The same goes for default fuel prices (fossil_fuel_oil_price_global), fleet age distributions, orderbooks, and feedstock availability.

How to build your own case#

To build your case from scratch using an AI assistant, we suggest to follow these steps

  1. Describe your case in a few sentences.

  2. Hand a start-up prompt to your AI assistant. Adapt some of our start-up prompts to your case and give it to your AI assistant.

  3. Answer questions from the AI assistant. Your answers decide the numbers the data library does not settle.

  4. Review the case sheet it shows you: one page with every model choice, every INVENTED number and every number you gave (GIVEN). Nothing is built before you approve it.

  5. Approve, let it build and run the deck.

  6. Check the results yourself and check if there is any errors

  7. Iterate and share what worked.

Step 1: Describe your case#

A few sentences are enough, if they pin down:

  • the types and quantity of ships in the simulation;

  • the trade or the region;

  • the years;

  • which fuels can be used, and where they could be made and bunkered;

  • which policy applies, and to which ships/fleets;

  • the question you want answered.

For Session 1’s case:

300 Panamax bulk carriers (about 80,000 DWT), owned by many companies, carry copper concentrate from San Antonio, Chile, to Rotterdam via the Panama Canal, loaded out and empty back, from 2026 to 2050. In 2026 they all burn fuel oil. They can switch to LNG, methanol or ammonia ships, and fit efficiency measures. E-methanol and e-ammonia can be made in Chile or in Europe, bio-methanol only in Europe. EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime cover half of the voyage. My question: which fuels do the owners choose, and when?

Step 2: Hand the start-up prompt to your assistant#

An AI assistant needs more context than a short description to build a deck that works. To make sure that this is captured successfully paste this start-up prompt into a new conversation with your AI coding assistant, with your case in place of the text in square brackets. If you already know which example you want to start from, say so in your case (for example: build it like Session 1’s copper trade).

I want to build and run a case with Navigate, the shipping decarbonisation model (https://github.com/zerocarbonshipping/navigate-zcs).

My case: [YOUR CASE, IN A FEW SENTENCES: which ships and how many, the trade or the region, the years, which fuels can be used, which regulation applies, and the question you want answered.]

1. Find Navigate on my machine (a folder usually named navigate-zcs) and work from its root, or ask me where it is; if it is not there, tell me. Check that it is installed (README.md) and tell me what is missing. Don't install anything yourself.
2. Read docs/workshop/building-a-deck.md first and follow it, then docs/reference_manual/, the example decks in simulations/examples/, the reference scenarios in simulations/scenarios/ and the data library in assumptions/. Take as your model the entry closest to my case in docs/workshop/prompt-bank.md: read its index first, which lists every entry by family, then only the entry you take, in full (for a variant, with the full prompt of the entry it names).
3. Before your first questions, tell me in two lines which family my case belongs to (a specific trade, a regional network, or a global case) and which entry you take as the model, and why; if none is close, say so. Then use the library's values wherever it has them, and ask me, a few questions at a time, about the rest. Keep the deck small.
4. Then show me a one-page case sheet and wait for my OK, writing no file before I give it: the decks and what separates them; the ships and how many; the routes or the region; the fuels and where they are made; each policy and which ships it covers; every number not from the library, marked INVENTED, or GIVEN if I gave it; and what you will report. Build in a new folder, simulations/user/my_simulation/ (my_simulation_2, _3, ... if taken), without changing any existing file.
5. Once I approve it, build and run the deck, and report as building-a-deck.md says.

The assistant first checks your installation and reads the repository. Then, before its first questions, it tells you in two lines which family (see Table below) your case belongs to and which tested example it takes as its model. For Session 1’s case it should answer: a specific trade; the prompt bank’s “Session 1’s copper trade”. If its choice looks wrong, say so now: it is the best moment to correct it.

The case families#

The assistant picks an example close to the case you would like to model. It picks from the repository’s prompt bank: a page of tested cases, each with the full prompt that built it and its test notes, grouped by family. You do not have to read it, since the assistant does, but a look at it tells you whether a case like yours already exists. The Examples pages show some example decks with their results.

Cases fall into three families of which decks are built in a similar way.

Fits a case about

If nothing fits to your case, the assistant starts from the closest example and says so. The bank also lists what the library does not cover (for example, no battery-electric ships): your assistant will tell you if your case needs INVENTED numbers (that you will have to update on your own to have a case that makes sense).

Step 3: Answer the questions from the AI assistant#

By default, the assistant takes number from the existing data library. It asks you, a few questions at a time, about the rest: how many ships, the distances, where the fuels are made, the level and the dates of a policy. If you do not know an answer, say so: the assistant proposes a value and marks it INVENTED. If you have numbers of your own, give them, even where the library has one: the assistant uses yours and marks them GIVEN, as below.

Using your own numbers instead of the Center’s#

The data library, assumptions/, holds the Center’s assumptions. You can replace any of them with your own for one simulation, without changing the library:

  • Give the numbers and say they are yours, in your case (Step 1) or when the assistant asks (Step 3): the value, its unit, the years it applies to, and where it comes from. For example: “Use our own fuel oil price instead of the library’s: [values by year], from [source].” or “The route is [distance] nm each way, from our voyage data.”

  • The assistant uses them as given, in your deck only. It marks each one GIVEN in a comment where it appears and lists them in the .nav header, next to the INVENTED ones. If one is far from the library’s value, it says so once, in the case sheet, but it does not replace it.

  • In Navigate’s units, or in your own: when the assistant asks for a number, it gives Navigate’s unit for it (for example a fuel price in USD per tonne; Navigate’s money is in US dollars of 2024); answering in that unit avoids any conversion. If your number is in another unit or currency (a fuel price in EUR per GJ), the assistant converts it and keeps your original number, each factor with its source (an exchange rate with its date) and the result: in the comment next to the number, in the .nav header and in the case sheet. Give the year of a price, and the exchange rate you want if you have one: money from another year is brought to 2024 dollars, with the price index written down the same way.

  • You are responsible for the conversion, as for the number: check each one in the case sheet, its factors and its result, and confirm it before the assistant builds.

  • Any number can be yours: a fleet’s size and ages, distances, speeds, a fuel price, a ship’s or a plant’s cost, a policy’s level and dates. You can even ask for a few ships rather than a market segment: Navigate then answers in fractions of ships.

  • Check them in the case sheet (Step 4) before you approve it.

  • To change one later, ask the assistant, or edit it yourself in the deck: it sits where its GIVEN comment is.

Your numbers are yours to source: the results follow them, so keep where each one came from. The prompt bank entries that rebuild examples 1 to 3 give every number of those decks this way.

Step 4: Review the case sheet#

Before building anything, the assistant shows you one page that says what it will build. Check five things before you approve it:

  • the question: is it the one you want answered?

  • the policies: which ships does each one cover, and on which part of the voyage?

  • every INVENTED number: would you defend it, or do you have a better source? If you do, give it: it becomes your own number;

  • every GIVEN number: is it the value you gave, and if it was converted, are the factors and the result right?

  • the folder: a new one under simulations/user/, so that nothing in the repository changes.

For Session 1’s case, the case sheet reads:

Show the case sheet

Case sheet: Session 1’s copper trade

  • Model: the prompt bank’s Session 1’s copper trade (family: a specific trade).

  • Deck: one deck, simulations/user/my_simulation/my_simulation.nav, 2026 to 2050 in yearly steps.

  • Ships: 300 Panamax bulk carriers of the library’s bulk_carrier_80k_dwt class (300 is INVENTED: about 8 % of the world fleet), all on fuel oil in 2026. The owners can order or convert to LNG, methanol and ammonia designs, and fit the class’s seven efficiency measures.

  • Route: San Antonio to Rotterdam via Panama, 7,600 nm each way (INVENTED), laden out and empty back; speed and port days from the class’s operational profile.

  • Fuels: fuel oil and LNG at the library’s world prices; e-methanol and e-ammonia made in Chile and in Europe, bio-methanol in Europe; all bunkerable at both ports.

  • Delivery to the ports: Chile to San Antonio 1,250 nm, Chile to Rotterdam and Europe to San Antonio 7,600 nm, Europe to Rotterdam 540 nm; 0.02 USD per tonne per nm; port handling 8 to 25 USD per tonne (all INVENTED).

  • Feedstock: 0.003 of shipping’s biogenic CO2, held at its 2026 level, and 0.01 of its gasifiable biomass (INVENTED).

  • Policy: the library’s EU ETS and FuelEU, with Rotterdam as their only port: they cover half of each voyage and Rotterdam’s port stays.

  • Report: the well-to-wake emissions in 2026 and 2050 and summed; the share of alternative fuels in 2050; the three biggest fuels.

The assistant builds from the case sheet and from the full prompt (from the prompt bank) of the example it took. Each full prompt starts by sending the assistant to the same page, Building a deck with an AI assistant. It gives the assistant what it cannot guess: where the syntax and the data library live, how to run a deck, and where the results come out as numbers. It is what lets any assistant (Claude, Copilot, ChatGPT, …) work from this repository, as long as it can read the repository’s files. The page also asks the assistant to end its report with what the page should have told it, so that it improves as more people build decks.

The full prompt behind Session 1’s case is in the prompt bank: Session 1’s copper trade.

Step 5: Build and run#

Once you approve the case sheet, the assistant writes the deck, runs it and reports: the exit code, the warnings and errors, and the results the case sheet promised. It fixes what the run reports before it tells you the deck works.

Running it again yourself#

In a terminal, in the Python environment where you installed Navigate, run from the repository root:

python -m navigate simulations/user/<your_case>/<your_case>.nav -d ./assumptions

<your_case> is the folder your prompt named: my_simulation for the start-up prompt.

Three things worth knowing:

  • python -m navigate from the repository root always runs this repository’s code. The shorter navigate simulations/... works too, but if another copy of Navigate is installed on your machine it may run that one instead, and fail on decks that are fine.

  • -d ./assumptions is needed whenever the deck uses Load, Import or Copy — which any deck built on the library does. A fully self-contained deck like the minimal single-vessel example of Tutorial 1 needs no -d at all.

  • Output lands next to the deck, not in your working directory. Navigate resolves the deck path first and writes everything relative to that.

Where the plots are#

Load DefaultPlot declares two Plot nodes, so a run fills two folders inside the deck’s own directory:

where

what is in it

<deck folder>/plots/

the headline set, nine charts — among them fuel consumed, absolute emissions, installed power, producer development

<deck folder>/plots_analysis/

the fuller set, thirty-two charts — among them fleet evolution and conversions, speeds, emission intensity, expenses, bunker price and supply per port, plant production costs

How many you get depends on what the deck contains. Two more files land beside them: <deck>.log, and plot_data.pkl, which holds the solved model.

Open the PNGs from the file browser, or in a notebook with from IPython.display import Image; display(Image(filename=...)). Which charts appear is set by the add_plot("...") calls in the deck; the full list of labels is in the appendix of the reference manual’s Plot page.

Old plots are not cleared

Navigate overwrites a chart it regenerates but never deletes one it no longer produces, so a PNG from an earlier configuration can sit in the folder looking exactly as current as the rest. Delete the folder before a re-run if you have changed which plots the deck asks for.

When the run finishes, check the Log summary table at the foot of <deck>.log: it counts warnings and errors, and is the quickest way to tell a clean run from one that merely completed.

Step 6: Check the results#

“It runs” says the syntax is right, not that the case is. Before trusting a result, check:

  • the run is clean: exit code 0, no errors, and only the warnings that come with the library;

  • the first year is plausible: a fleet on fuel oil emits about 92 gCO2e per MJ well-to-wake, and the energy it uses matches its size;

  • a switch is a switch: judge it from the energy consumed by fuel, not from the number of ships, because methanol and ammonia ships can run entirely on their oil pilot fuel;

  • a bunker price of 0 means no supply, not a free fuel;

  • the answer moves the right way: a stronger push away from fossil fuels does not burn more of them.

For Session 1’s case, compare with the workshop’s own deck: 5,935 kt CO2e in 2026, 3,541 kt in 2050, about 127,800 kt over 2026-2050, and 38.5 % alternative fuels in 2050, with fuel oil, e-ammonia and LNG the three biggest fuels. A deck rebuilt from the prompt bank’s full prompt came within 0.2 % of these.

Step 7: Iterate, and share what worked#

Change one thing at a time. Edit the .inc files of your deck, or ask the assistant to, and run it again. To build a different case from the same pattern, change what differs in your case description (Step 1): the route, the ships, where the fuels are made, or which regulation applies. Starting from Session 1’s case, for example:

  • another route, with no regulation: “200 Suezmax crude tankers (about 127,000 DWT) carry crude oil from Ras Tanura, Saudi Arabia, to Ningbo, China, laden out and in ballast home, from 2026 to 2050. They start on fuel oil and can switch to LNG, e-ammonia or e-methanol made in the Middle East. No regulation applies.” The prompt bank’s One trade, two ports is this case, written as a variant of Session 1’s copper trade.

  • other ships and fuel supply: “150 Capesize bulk carriers (about 200,000 DWT) carry iron ore from Tubarão, Brazil, to Qingdao, China. E-ammonia and e-methanol can be made in Brazil or in China.”

  • the same trade under stricter rules: “… EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime cover the whole voyage, not only half of it.”

  • the same trade with a global policy as well: “… and a global fuel standard applies from 2028, as in Session 2’s mid-regulation scenario.”

Ask for an explanation notebook. When the deck runs, the assistant offers to write a notebook for your case, laid out like Session 1: a map or a diagram of the case on top, the results as figures (emissions, freight rate and fuel mix over time, each deck compared with the others), and a cell of levers to change your inputs and see what moves.

Share your case. When the deck runs, the assistant ends its report with a prompt bank entry: the model it took, your case sheet written as the differences from that model, the test result, and what the guide should have told it. Paste it into a new issue on the repository. The workshop authors add it to the prompt bank and fold its notes into the guide, so that the next person’s deck works first time more often.

Other families#

The same seven steps build a regional or a global case. These two show what such a case looks like; their full prompts and test notes are in the prompt bank.

Regional network: Caribbean shipping#

A region’s own ships, and rules that do not reach them yet. 330 domestic and intra-Caribbean ships (tugs, ferries, general cargo ships and small tankers), nearly all under 5,000 GT, and 40 container feeders on a Miami - Jamaica - Trinidad - Martinique loop, 2026 to 2050. New fuels are made in Trinidad, on the US Gulf Coast or in Chile, and shipped in. Three decks: no policy; today’s rules, a global fuel standard as drafted at the IMO plus the EU ETS and FuelEU with Martinique as the EU port, which cover the feeders only; and the same rules extended to the regional ships. The question is how the region’s own ships are affected, today and if the rules reached them.

Built on public data, with gaps. The fleet counts come from the IMO CARES report on domestic shipping (2024). The split of the cargo ships, the distances, speeds, delivery costs and the region’s share of plant building are INVENTED, and the prompt marks each one. The regional fleet is not calibrated: with the library’s world-average ships it burns more fuel than CARES reports, so compare the decks with each other, not with the region’s real totals.

Closest to your case if you study a region with its own small ships and international ships calling at its ports, fuels made at several sites and shipped in, or a policy that covers some ships and not others. Full prompt in the prompt bank.

Three Caribbean ports and Miami: the feeders’ loop, the regional loop, and fuels shipped in from three sites.

Global case: Session 2’s reference scenario#

The global case of Session 2, rebuilt from scratch. The whole international fleet, as the data library describes it, from 2026 to 2050 on Session 2’s coarse grid, under EU ETS, FuelEU and a moderate global fuel standard: the workshop_basecase_mid_regulation scenario.

Closest to your case if you study the world fleet or a global policy, built from the library’s modules and reference scenarios rather than node by node. Full prompt in the prompt bank.
